# Presentation Best Practices

## Overview

This skill encodes best practices for creating effective slide-based presentations, synthesised from authoritative public sources (MIT, McKinsey, Penn State, Duarte) and Atlassian's internal AI slides standards (Malleable Content team, hello.atlassian.net, 2026).

Apply these principles when:
- Creating a new slide deck or adding slides to an existing one
- Reviewing or critiquing slides for quality
- Rewriting or improving existing slide content
- Structuring a presentation narrative from scratch

---

## 1. Narrative Structure

### Lead with the answer (Pyramid Principle)
Structure presentations top-down: state your conclusion or recommendation first, then support it. Audiences — especially executives — want the answer before the evidence, not after.

- **Wrong**: Build up evidence → reveal conclusion at end
- **Right**: State conclusion → provide supporting evidence → elaborate detail

### Use SCQA to frame the opening
Before presenting your answer, orient the audience with:
- **Situation** — the agreed-upon context ("We're building Forge apps for Atlassian customers...")
- **Complication** — what has changed or why action is needed now ("AI agents can now build complete apps autonomously...")
- **Question** — the question the audience naturally asks ("So how should we approach AI-assisted Forge development?")
- **Answer** — your recommendation or thesis ("Here's a practical framework...")

### Structure the arc
A well-structured presentation follows a clear arc:
1. Hook — open with impact (striking fact, provocative question, brief story)
2. Context — SCQA framing
3. Body — supporting evidence, organised logically
4. Summary — reinforce key takeaways (rule of three)
5. Call to action — clear, specific, actionable next step
6. Q&A — before the final slide, not at the very end
7. Close — end on the key message, not on Q&A

## 2. Slide Content Principles

### One idea per slide
Each slide should propose or answer **one primary question**. If you can't summarise the slide's point in a single sentence, split it into two slides.

### Use action titles, not topic labels
Slide titles should be **complete sentences that state the conclusion**, not generic labels.

| ❌ Topic title | ✅ Action title |
|---|---|
| Market Analysis | AI coding agents reached $2B ARR in February 2026 |
| Results | Forge apps built with AI take hours, not weeks |
| Next Steps | Start with the Forge MCP Server today — zero install required |

Reading the titles alone should tell the story of the entire presentation.

### Minimise text on slides
Slides support your spoken words — they are not a transcript. Guidelines:
- **3–5 bullet points maximum** per slide; often fewer is better
- Bullets are **fragments, not paragraphs** — one idea per bullet
- If the audience is reading, they are not listening
- Favour visuals, diagrams, and tables over walls of text

### The "So What?" test
After every slide, ask: *"So what? Why does this matter to my audience?"* If you cannot immediately answer, revise or cut the slide.

### The Glance Test
Each slide should be understandable within 3 seconds at a glance. If it takes longer, simplify.

### Claims must be grounded
Every claim should feel supported — by data, a concrete example, a comparison, or a direct quote. Unsubstantiated assertions erode credibility.

## 3. Visual Design

### Whitespace is not wasted space
Whitespace gives content structure, balance, and breathing room. Cluttered slides signal poor thinking. Leave generous margins and padding.

### Colour restraint
- Use **2–3 colours maximum** — a primary, an accent, and a neutral
- Maintain a **4.5:1 minimum contrast ratio** between text and background (WCAG AA)
- Do not use colour as the only way to convey meaning (accessibility)
- Apply colours consistently — the same colour should always mean the same thing

### Typography
- Use **sans-serif fonts** (Arial, Helvetica, Roboto, Montserrat) for on-screen readability
- Use **no more than 2 font families** per deck
- Apply size and weight hierarchy: title > heading > body > caption
- Do not emphasise everything — if everything is highlighted, nothing is

### Visual hierarchy
Guide the eye deliberately: the most important element should be largest, highest contrast, or most prominent. Everything else is subordinate.

### Consistency
Apply all design choices (fonts, colours, layout, icon style) consistently across every slide. Inconsistency distracts the audience from the content.

## 4. Data Visualisation

### Choose the right format

| Use a **chart** when... | Use a **table** when... |
|---|---|
| You want to show a trend or pattern | Precise values matter more than the shape |
| The "shape" of the data tells the story | The data has multiple different units |
| You need to reduce cognitive load quickly | Users need to look up specific figures |
| Executive summary or trend analysis | Detailed reference or comparison |

### Simplify ruthlessly
Show only the data that supports the slide's one idea. Remove gridlines, unnecessary axes, and decorative elements. Label data points directly rather than using a legend where possible.

### Never mislead
Axes must start at zero for bar charts. Truncated axes distort perception. Always show the data honestly.

## 5. Speaker Notes

### What to include
Speaker notes complement the slide — they do not repeat it. Good speaker notes contain:
- The key talking point(s) for this slide in natural spoken language
- Statistics, quotes, or examples too detailed for the slide itself
- Transition cues ("This brings us to the next point...")
- Prompts for stories or audience interaction
- Context for co-presenters or anyone presenting the deck without you

### What to avoid
- Word-for-word transcripts of what you plan to say (these become a crutch)
- Repetition of exactly what is written on the slide
- Jargon or abbreviations you might misread under pressure

### Length
Aim for **3–8 sentences or bullet points** per slide. Long enough to be useful; short enough to scan quickly while presenting.

## 6. Pacing and Length

### Slide count
- Rule of thumb: **1 slide per minute** of presentation time
- For deep technical content: allow 2–5 minutes per slide
- For rapid visual storytelling: multiple slides per minute is fine
- Use only as many slides as needed — no filler, no padding

### Speaking pace
- **120–150 words per minute** is optimal for comprehension and retention
- Slower than natural conversation — strategic pauses convey confidence
- The fix for feeling rushed is cutting content, not speaking faster

### Guy Kawasaki's 10/20/30 rule (for pitches)
10 slides · 20 minutes · 30pt minimum font size. A useful heuristic for keeping decks focused.

## 7. Opening and Closing

### Opening — the first 60 seconds are critical
Capture attention immediately. Do not open with:
- "Thank you for having me"
- An agenda slide
- Your biography

Do open with:
- A striking statistic or surprising fact
- A provocative question directed at the audience
- A brief, relevant story
- A bold claim you will then prove

### Closing — leave with the key message
- Summarise with the **rule of three** — three takeaways maximum
- End with a **clear, specific call to action** ("Add the Forge MCP Server to your AI tool today")
- Place Q&A *before* the final slide so you close on your terms, not on a random question
- The last thing the audience hears and sees should be your most important point

## 8. Quality Checks

Before finalising any slide deck, run these checks:

### The title read-through test
Read only the slide titles in order. They should tell a coherent, logical story on their own. If they don't, revise until they do.

### The "So What?" test
For every slide: can you immediately state why it matters to your audience? If not, revise or cut.

### The Glance Test
Can each slide be understood in 3 seconds? If not, simplify.

### Faithfulness check
Does every slide accurately represent its source material? Have you introduced any claims not supported by the evidence? Correct before presenting.

### Accessibility check
- Contrast ratio ≥ 4.5:1 for all text
- Colour is not the only differentiator
- Font sizes readable at a glance (minimum 24pt recommended for body)
- Alt text or verbal description provided for images containing information

## 9. Atlassian-Specific Standards

When working on this project's `Presentation.md` (which uses a markdown slide format), also apply:

- **Format**: Follow the conventions in `PresentationFormat.md` exactly
- **Tone**: "Professional and respectful; assume intelligent, time-constrained viewers" (Atlassian Malleable Content standard)
- **Voice**: Active voice; optimise for clarity, not completeness
- **Titles**: Complete thoughts (action titles), not labels
- **Speaker notes**: Detailed enough for someone to present the deck without prior context — include talking points, background, and anticipated questions
- **Diagrams**: Use ASCII art diagrams where visual structure aids comprehension (appropriate for markdown format)
- **Tables**: Use for comparisons; keep to 4–6 columns maximum for readability
- **Consistency**: Apply formatting conventions uniformly across all slides
